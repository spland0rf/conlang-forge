## Short common words (owner decision)
Pronouns, articles/determiners, conjunctions, negation/answers, question words, prepositions, auxiliaries and a few adverbs are so frequent that real languages make them cheap: mostly one syllable, and they own most of the one- and two-letter words.
- **The list** is `conlang_forge/smallwords.py` (about 100 base words in 8 classes, each with a probability of being efficient: about 0.95 for the most basic such as and, of, the, I, not, to; 0.8-0.9 for common ones; 0.35-0.65 for longer ones such as beneath, between, although). Derived words (me, my, him, she, these, whom, there, off, out ...) and compounds (into, within) are not listed: they inherit shortness from their base plus an affix.
- **The roll** per word is a pure function of (seed, word), so a vocabulary upgrade cannot change it; efficient words get a one-syllable form of at most 3 phonemes (weighted 3x toward one- and two-letter forms), allocated before every other word, most frequent first. Families of short words (this/that/here/now, who/what/which, when/where, why/how) get a short stem and keep their members to one syllable by changing a sound rather than adding one.
- **One- and two-letter forms are kept for them:** ordinary stems, family members and free roots may not use them, except with a 5% per-word/stem chance (a leak). Across test seeds 80-100% of such forms in a language are function words.
- Two short words may differ by one similar sound (ta/da); this is not counted as a near pair (every other pair is). Forms that are readable as another word + affix are still repaired as before.
- **Capacity limit, not a bug:** a language with few possible syllables (for example CV only with 3 vowels) has too few one-syllable forms, and its affixes already use most of them. There the short words get the lightest two-syllable forms (about 25% one-syllable, average 2.0 syllables against 3.1 for other words). Typical languages reach 75-90% one-syllable.
- The one-syllable stem budget for ordinary families now counts only forms of three or more letters, so a CV-only language has no one-syllable ordinary stems.
